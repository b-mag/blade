# Policy 300 — Use of Force

> Kestrel County Sheriff's Office Policy Manual · Effective 2025-03-01 · Revised 2026-06-15
> FICTIONAL POLICY FOR DEMONSTRATION PURPOSES ONLY

## 300.1 Purpose and Scope
This policy sets the standards that govern when and how members of the Kestrel County Sheriff's Office (KCSO) may use force while carrying out their duties. The Office recognizes that the authority to use force is one of the most serious powers entrusted to a deputy, and that the community's confidence depends on that authority being exercised with restraint, sound judgment, and respect for human life.

This policy applies to all sworn deputies, detention deputies assigned to the Kestrel County Detention Center, reserve deputies, and any other member authorized to carry a weapon or control device on behalf of the Office. It should be read together with Policy 302 (Duty to Intercede and Report), Policy 303 (Control Devices and Less-Lethal Tools), Policy 306 (Handcuffing and Restraints), and Policy 330 (Crisis Intervention and Mental Health Response).

Nothing in this policy is intended to create a higher legal standard of care than that required by law. It is intended to establish the expectations of Sheriff Dana Whitlock and the Office for the conduct of its members, and violations may result in discipline independent of any criminal or civil outcome.

## 300.2 Definitions
**Force** — Any physical effort, technique, tool, or weapon used to compel compliance or overcome resistance, other than unresisted handcuffing, escorting, or searching.

**Deadly force** — Force that a reasonable person would understand creates a substantial risk of causing death or serious bodily injury, including the intentional discharge of a firearm toward a person.

**Serious bodily injury** — Injury that creates a substantial risk of death, causes serious permanent disfigurement, or results in prolonged loss or impairment of any bodily organ or function.

**Imminent** — A threat is imminent when, based on the totality of the circumstances, a reasonable deputy would believe the person has the present ability, opportunity, and apparent intent to immediately cause death or serious bodily injury. Imminent does not mean the harm has already begun, but it is more than a mere fear of future harm.

**De-escalation** — Actions and communication intended to reduce the intensity of an encounter and the likelihood that force will be needed, such as slowing the pace, creating distance, using cover, and calm verbal engagement.

**Feasible** — Reasonably capable of being done under the circumstances without unreasonably increasing the danger to the deputy or others.

**Totality of the circumstances** — All facts known to the deputy at the time force is used, including the conduct of the deputy and the subject leading up to the use of force.

## 300.3 Use of Force
Deputies may use only the force that is objectively reasonable to accomplish a lawful purpose, such as making an arrest, preventing an escape, overcoming resistance, or protecting themselves or others from harm. Force must end or be reduced as soon as the subject's resistance ends or the threat subsides. The subsections below describe how deputies are expected to evaluate, select, and limit the force they use.

### 300.3.1 Objectively Reasonable Force
The Office follows the objective reasonableness standard articulated by the United States Supreme Court in Graham v. Connor. Under this standard, a use of force is judged from the perspective of a reasonable deputy on the scene, with the information that deputy had at the moment, rather than with the benefit of hindsight. The analysis recognizes that deputies are often required to make split-second decisions in situations that are tense, uncertain, and rapidly changing.

Objective reasonableness is not measured by the deputy's intent or motivation. A deputy acting with good intentions may still use unreasonable force, and an objectively reasonable use of force is not made improper by an improper motive, although that motive may still violate other Office policies.

In applying this standard, deputies should consider at minimum the severity of the crime at issue, whether the subject poses an immediate threat to the safety of deputies or others, and whether the subject is actively resisting or attempting to evade arrest by flight. Force that is reasonable at one moment may become unreasonable the next as circumstances change, and deputies are responsible for continually reassessing.

### 300.3.2 Totality of the Circumstances
When deciding whether to use force and what type of force to use, deputies must weigh the totality of the circumstances known or reasonably perceived at the time. Relevant factors include, but are not limited to:

- The seriousness of the suspected offense and the reason for the contact.
- The immediacy and severity of the threat to deputies or the public.
- Whether the subject is armed, and the type of weapon and its proximity.
- The subject's apparent age, size, physical ability, and condition compared to the deputy's.
- Signs of mental illness, intellectual disability, intoxication, medical distress, or a language barrier that may affect the subject's ability to understand or comply.
- The number of subjects and deputies present and the availability of backup.
- Environmental factors such as lighting, terrain, traffic, crowds, and available cover.
- Whether the subject has been restrained or has stopped resisting.
- The availability and likely effectiveness of other options, including time and distance.

No single factor controls the analysis. Deputies are expected to be able to articulate the specific facts that made their decision reasonable.

### 300.3.3 De-Escalation Requirements
When safe and feasible, deputies shall use de-escalation tactics before using force and throughout an encounter. The goal of de-escalation is to gain voluntary compliance and to give the subject the opportunity to make better decisions, reducing the need for force and the risk of injury to everyone involved.

De-escalation techniques may include:

- Using time, distance, and cover to slow the encounter and create options.
- Communicating calmly, clearly, and respectfully, and allowing the subject a reasonable opportunity to comply.
- Assigning one deputy to communicate to avoid conflicting commands.
- Requesting additional resources, such as a supervisor, Crisis Intervention Team (CIT) deputy, interpreter, or less-lethal capability.
- Containing the subject and the scene rather than forcing immediate resolution when no one is in imminent danger.

De-escalation is not required when it would place the deputy or others at unreasonable risk, and a deputy is never required to retreat or yield in the face of an imminent threat. However, deputies should avoid tactics that unnecessarily create a situation in which force becomes necessary, and supervisors will consider a deputy's pre-force decision-making when reviewing an incident.

### 300.3.4 Levels of Resistance and Proportional Response
The Office uses the following categories to describe a subject's behavior. These categories help deputies select a proportional response; they are not a rigid ladder, and deputies may move directly to any reasonable option the circumstances require.

- **Passive resistance** — The subject does not follow lawful commands but takes no physical action to resist, such as going limp, refusing to stand, or sitting down. Appropriate responses generally include verbal direction, warnings, escort holds, and lifting or carrying techniques.
- **Active resistance** — The subject uses physical movement to avoid control without attempting to harm the deputy, such as pulling away, bracing, or running. Appropriate responses may include control holds, takedowns, and, where authorized by Policy 303, certain control devices.
- **Assaultive resistance** — The subject attempts to cause or causes bodily injury to the deputy or another person, such as striking, kicking, or wrestling for control. Appropriate responses may include personal weapons, impact tools, conducted energy weapons, and other less-lethal options.
- **Life-threatening resistance** — The subject's actions create an imminent threat of death or serious bodily injury. Deadly force may be authorized under Section 300.4.

The level of force must be reduced as the subject's resistance decreases.

### 300.3.5 Non-Compliant and Resistive Subjects
A subject's failure to follow commands does not, by itself, justify the use of force. Deputies should first consider why the person may not be complying. Possible reasons include a medical condition, mental-health crisis, hearing impairment, limited English proficiency, intoxication, fear, or a simple failure to understand what is being asked.

When a subject is non-compliant but not posing a threat, deputies should take reasonable steps to gain compliance through communication, repeating commands in a clear and simple manner, and allowing reasonable time to respond. Where an immediate arrest is not necessary, deputies may consider alternatives such as delaying the arrest, obtaining a warrant, or requesting additional resources.

When a subject is actively or assaultively resisting, deputies should use coordinated team tactics where possible, because multiple deputies working together can often achieve control with less force and fewer injuries than a single deputy. Once the subject is under control, deputies shall immediately transition to restraint and recovery procedures, place the subject in a position that does not restrict breathing, and monitor the subject as described in Section 300.6 and Policy 306.

### 300.3.6 Subjects Armed with Edged Weapons
Encounters with subjects armed with knives or other edged or cutting instruments are dangerous because these weapons can cause fatal injuries quickly at close range. At the same time, many such encounters involve people experiencing a mental-health crisis, suicidal thoughts, or intoxication, and the subject may not intend to harm anyone other than themselves. Deputies should approach these situations with the goal of resolving them safely through distance, time, and communication.

When feasible, deputies responding to a subject armed with an edged weapon should:

- Maintain distance and use cover or barriers, recognizing the reactionary gap — the time a deputy needs to perceive and respond to a sudden attack. Greater distance provides more time and more options.
- Avoid unnecessarily closing distance or approaching the subject, and avoid tactics that leave the deputy without an avenue to reposition.
- Give clear, calm verbal commands, with one designated deputy communicating, and allow reasonable time for compliance.
- Request a supervisor and a CIT deputy, and stage less-lethal options such as a 40mm launcher or conducted energy weapon with an assigned lethal cover deputy.
- Consider whether the subject appears to be in crisis and whether containment and time could allow the situation to resolve without force.

The presence of a knife alone does not justify deadly force. Deadly force is authorized only when the subject's actions create an imminent threat of death or serious bodily injury as described in Section 300.4.1.

### 300.3.7 Restricted Techniques
Carotid restraints, chokeholds, and any technique that applies pressure to the neck or throat in a way that may restrict blood flow or breathing are prohibited unless deadly force would be authorized. Deputies shall not kneel or apply sustained body weight on a subject's neck, head, or back after the subject is controlled.

Deputies shall not use force to punish, retaliate, or coerce statements, and shall not use force against a person who is handcuffed or otherwise restrained except as necessary to prevent imminent injury, escape, or the destruction of evidence, and then only to the degree objectively reasonable. Striking a subject's head with an impact weapon is considered deadly force.

## 300.4 Deadly Force
Deadly force is the most serious action a deputy can take. Its use is governed by the criteria below, and it shall be used only as a last resort when other reasonable options are not available or would not be effective. Deputies must consider the risk to bystanders before using deadly force.

### 300.4.1 Deadly Force Criteria
A deputy may use deadly force only when the deputy reasonably believes it is necessary to:

- Protect the deputy or another person from an imminent threat of death or serious bodily injury; or
- Apprehend a fleeing person for a felony that threatened or resulted in death or serious bodily injury, when the deputy reasonably believes the person will cause death or serious bodily injury to another unless immediately apprehended.

Deadly force shall not be used against a person who poses a danger only to themselves, solely to prevent the destruction of property, or solely to prevent the escape of a person who does not meet the criteria above. Deputies shall not fire warning shots. Deputies shall stop using deadly force as soon as the imminent threat ends, and shall reassess after each application.

The fact that a subject is fleeing, is non-compliant, or possesses a weapon is not by itself sufficient to justify deadly force. The deputy must be able to articulate the specific facts that made the threat imminent.

### 300.4.2 Warnings Before Deadly Force
When feasible, a deputy shall identify themselves as a law enforcement officer and give a verbal warning that deadly force may be used before using it. The warning should be clear and simple, such as a command to drop the weapon, and should be given in a manner the subject can reasonably be expected to hear and understand.

A warning is not required when giving it would increase the danger to the deputy or others, or when the threat is so immediate that there is no reasonable opportunity to give one. Deputies should document in their report whether a warning was given and, if not, why it was not feasible.

### 300.4.3 Shooting At or From Moving Vehicles
Shooting at a moving vehicle is rarely effective and creates significant risk to bystanders, passengers, and deputies, because disabling the driver may leave an uncontrolled vehicle in motion. Deputies shall not discharge a firearm at or from a moving vehicle unless deadly force is otherwise authorized under Section 300.4.1 and the threat comes from something other than the vehicle itself, such as an occupant firing a weapon.

Deputies shall not intentionally place themselves in the path of a moving vehicle, and shall move out of the path of an approaching vehicle whenever possible rather than firing. A vehicle being used as a weapon may justify deadly force only when there is no reasonable opportunity to move to safety and the deputy reasonably believes the vehicle presents an imminent threat of death or serious bodily injury to the deputy or others.

## 300.5 Duty to Intervene
Any deputy who observes another member using force that is clearly beyond what is objectively reasonable shall, when in a position to do so, intervene to prevent or stop the force. Intervention may be verbal or physical. The observing deputy shall promptly report the incident to a supervisor. These obligations apply regardless of the rank or seniority of the member using force and are described in detail in Policy 302 (Duty to Intercede and Report).

## 300.6 Medical Aid After Force
After any use of force, deputies shall assess the subject for injury once the scene is safe. Deputies shall promptly render first aid within the scope of their training and request emergency medical services when a subject is injured, complains of injury, appears to be in medical distress, or has been subjected to a conducted energy weapon probe deployment, impact munition strike, or neck restraint.

Subjects shall be placed in a recovery position as soon as practical after being restrained and shall not be left face-down. Deputies shall continuously monitor the subject's breathing, consciousness, and color, paying particular attention to signs of respiratory distress, excited delirium-type symptoms, or drug intoxication. A subject who refuses medical care shall still be monitored, and the refusal shall be documented. Any person transported to the Kestrel County Detention Center after a use of force shall be identified to booking staff so that medical screening can occur.

## 300.7 Reporting the Use of Force
All uses of force, other than unresisted handcuffing or escorting, shall be documented accurately and completely. Reports shall describe the subject's actions, the deputy's perceptions, the force used, the reasons for the force, and any injuries, in specific factual terms consistent with Policy 602 (Report Preparation).

### 300.7.1 Supervisor Notification
A deputy who uses force, or who witnesses a use of force, shall notify an on-duty supervisor as soon as practical and in all cases before the end of the deputy's shift. Immediate notification is required when the force results in injury or a complaint of injury, involves a firearm discharge, a conducted energy weapon, an impact weapon, a 40mm launcher, or a canine bite, or when the subject alleges misconduct. Deputies involved in a deadly force incident shall follow the notifications and procedures of the Office's officer-involved critical incident protocol and shall not complete a written report until directed by the investigating authority.

### 300.7.2 Use of Force Report
Each deputy who uses force shall complete a Use of Force report in the records management system before the end of shift unless the supervisor approves a delay because of injury or other exceptional circumstances. Witnessing deputies shall complete a supplemental report. The report shall include:

- The facts that led to the contact and the reason for the use of force.
- The subject's behavior and level of resistance.
- De-escalation efforts attempted, or why they were not feasible.
- Each type of force used and the subject's response.
- Injuries observed or claimed and the medical aid provided.
- Whether body-worn camera footage exists, consistent with Policy 342.

## 300.8 Supervisor Responsibilities
A supervisor notified of a use of force shall respond to the scene when the force resulted in injury, involved a weapon or control device, or when requested. The supervisor shall ensure the subject has received medical attention, identify and interview the subject and witnesses where feasible, photograph injuries and the scene, and ensure that body-worn camera recordings and other evidence are preserved.

The supervisor shall review all related reports and recordings and complete a supervisory review within 72 hours, documenting whether the force appears consistent with policy, training, and tactics. Any apparent policy violation shall be forwarded to the District Commander and the Professional Standards Unit. A supervisor who was involved in the use of force shall not conduct the review, which shall be assigned to another supervisor.

## 300.9 Administrative Review Board
The Use of Force Review Board reviews all deadly force incidents, all uses of force resulting in serious bodily injury, all in-custody deaths, and any other incident referred by the Sheriff or a Division Commander. The Board is composed of a Captain appointed by the Sheriff as chair, a Lieutenant from a district not involved in the incident, the Training Unit supervisor, and a deputy-rank member with current defensive tactics instructor certification.

The Board evaluates the incident for compliance with policy, the quality of tactics and decision-making before and during the force, supervision, training needs, and equipment issues. The Board issues a written finding to the Sheriff that the force was within policy, within policy with training or tactical concerns, or not within policy. The Board does not determine criminal liability, and its review generally occurs after any criminal investigation is complete.

## 300.10 Training and Data Review
All sworn members shall receive annual training on this policy, including de-escalation, crisis intervention principles, the duty to intervene, edged weapon encounters, and scenario-based decision-making. Members shall demonstrate proficiency before being authorized to carry any weapon or control device.

The Professional Standards Unit shall prepare an annual analysis of use of force data, including trends by district, type of force, subject demographics, and injuries, to identify training, policy, or equipment needs. A summary of this analysis shall be made available to the public, with personal identifying information removed.
